# Exploded view

Read this when the learner's gap is about structure: what something is made of, in which order, and why each part is there. The object is pulled apart along the axis it was assembled on, so the order of the parts is the explanation. Every part has a job; a part without one is cut.

This is a Workbench mode. Everything in [workbench.md](workbench.md) applies: the learning boundary, glass, motion, spacing and delivery. This file adds only what is specific to exploded views. The kit in [exploded-view-kit.js](../assets/exploded-view-kit.js) implements the camera, the renderer, picking and the part popover; copy it inline into the output so the file stays standalone. Start a new exploded view from [keyboard-switch.html](../examples/keyboard-switch.html): keep its `<head>` styles and the inlined kit unchanged, and replace only the title, intro and the parts script at the end. [keyboard-switch.html](../examples/keyboard-switch.html) (vertical stack), [limo-door.html](../examples/limo-door.html) (horizontal, through the door's thickness) and [macbook-pro.html](../examples/macbook-pro.html) (mixed: a tilted lid stack and a vertical base, per-part offsets) are the reference examples. The docked toolbar, popover, spacing and motion follow every Workbench guardrail; this mode has no exceptions. The keyboard legend sits in the header area in the upper third, not under the toolbar.

## Plan it

1. **Truth table first.** Before any geometry, write the parts as data: name, one-sentence job, real dimensions and the one or two numbers a learner would ask about (travel, force, thickness, capacity). Use typical values from a datasheet or standard and say "typical" where they vary. The geometry, the popover and the readout all read this table.
2. **Pick the assembly axis.** Parts move apart the way they were put together: `vertical` for stacks (a key switch, a phone, a PCB), `horizontal` for things assembled side by side or layered through their thickness (a flashlight, a drivetrain, an armoured door, the layers of a display), `radial` for things built around a centre (a motor, layers of a cable), or a per-part vector when the real disassembly is mixed. The axis is a choice about meaning, not about fitting the frame.
3. **Explode far enough to see into every part.** In the exploded state the clear gap between two neighbours along the axis is 0.6 × the larger of their footprints (the width across the axis), so from any elevation on the L-curve down to about 30° you can see the top of the lower part and the underside of the upper one (a stem's cross, a socket, a spring seat). Compute offsets cumulatively from each part's real extent, not from a fixed share of the total height. Plates (thinner along the axis than a quarter of their smaller face side) are the exception: in a stack where most parts are plates, the gap next to a plate is 0.2 × the plate's smaller face side, so a stack of large panels opens at 45° without stretching into a corridor.
4. **5 to 12 parts.** Fewer and nothing is learned; more and the learner stops reading. Merge screws and clips into the part they hold unless the fastener is the lesson.
5. **Order is reading order.** Parts are listed in assembly order. Up and Down in the popover walk that order.

## Draw it

- Build parts from the kit's primitives (box, rounded box, cylinder, frustum, ring, helix, extruded convex polygon) placed in millimetres, in real proportions. Small true details (a spring's turns, a stem's cross, pins) carry more than surface decoration.
- Parts read as drawn objects: flat-ish shading plus thin crease lines on every edge sharper than 30°, so a grey box never dissolves into a grey blob.
- The object always fills the view and is never cut off: fit the camera to the assembly's swept envelope (its projected bounds sampled over a full turn at the current elevation and explode amount), so it fills about 60 % of the free height in the overview, sits centred in the free space by its screen bounds rather than its 3D centre, and does not breathe while it spins. The L-curve scales that fitted distance instead of using fixed units.
- No coplanar faces and no z-fighting: holes, discs and decals are real geometry offset at least 0.05 mm from the surface they sit on, and an opaque layer that must hide a detailed layer behind it (a cover glass over a touch grid) sits at least 0.5 mm in front of it, drawn with polygon offset, and the near/far ratio stays at or below 1 : 1000. Check by scrolling slowly through the whole L-curve with the part closest to a surface open.
- Every part keeps its contrast in both themes: its lightness differs from the ground by at least 0.15 (OKLab L). The kit maps the material tiers per theme (dark theme L' = 0.38 + (L − 0.30) · 0.93, light theme L' = 0.22 + (L − 0.30) · 0.97), so a dark keycap is mid-dark grey on the dark ground and a clear sleeve is mid-light grey on the light ground.
- Stacks of plates (layers that are thin along the explode axis: armour, glass laminates, display layers) start with the explode axis at about 45° to the view direction (default yaw ±45°), so every plate shows its face and the gaps between plates are visible. Never start a plate stack edge-on.
- Long horizontal rows (length more than twice their other extents) start with the row near screen-horizontal (default yaw −15°, compact objects −35°) and are fitted to their bounds at that default yaw, so they use the screen's width; while turning, their ends may pass under the docked bars. Compact objects are fitted to their yaw-swept envelope so their size does not change while turning.
- **The scene is a seamless studio cove.** Floor and background are one colour, the page ground, with no edge anywhere: no finite floor disc, no horizon line, no gradient that ends. In light theme that ground is near white (`#fafafa`, L 0.985), in dark theme the usual dark ground. The only things that mark the floor are the plinth and the contact shadow, so at every elevation and zoom the object stands in a finished space. The camera never goes below the plinth top (eye height ≥ plinth top + 2 % of the camera distance), so nothing is ever seen from underneath.
- The object stands on a visible plinth: always a true circle, never an ellipse or a shape stretched to the object's footprint. It is centred on the turn axis under the lowest part, with radius r = max(0.4 × the largest dimension of the exploded assembly's bounding box, 1.1 × the largest horizontal distance of any part from the axis); long rows use 0.35 × their length instead and let the exploded ends overhang. Its size never changes while turning or tilting. It is a low cylinder (height 0.02 × r, at least 4 mm) drawn in flat, unlit colours, because lighting turns a light plinth grey: top ground L + 0.06 in dark and − 0.045 in light, side band + 0.025 / − 0.09, a crease line on its top edge and the contact shadow of the lowest part on it. Read the ground's L as the cube root of its luminance (the exact OKLab L of a neutral grey), never from an approximation. Check it in a screenshot of both themes: the plinth top must differ from the surrounding floor by at least 0.05 L. An object floating with no floor or plinth is a defect.
- Spatial order equals list order. Along the explode direction, part n+1 sits next to part n, so Up and Down always move the camera to a neighbour, never across the whole object. Choose the order so parts of similar shape are not adjacent where an alternative reading order is equally true.
- **It looks like the thing that was asked for.** Someone who knows the object recognises the assembled model at a glance from its silhouette and its signature features: a monitor has its stand and the logo on its back, a car door has its window frame, handle and emblem, a keycap has its sculpted, tapered profile. Build the outline from the real shape (convex extrusions, rounded corners, slanted edges), not from a box with the right size. Give each part its one or two telling details (holes, slots, ribs, a logo, a hinge) and stop there: details that identify, not decoration.
- **Neighbours never look alike.** Two parts next to each other must differ by more than thickness: a different lightness tier, a different finish (gloss or matte), or a structural feature that says what the part is. Prefer the real structure: armour ceramic as a grid of tiles, dual-hardness steel as two sub-layers, laminated glass with its thin interlayer films, optical films as a fanned stack of separate sheets, an LCD with its driver flex cables along one edge, a circuit board with chips and connectors, a speaker with its cones. Check it in the exploded state: cover the labels and every part must still be told apart from its neighbours.
- **Assembled means assembled.** In the assembled state the outer parts close the object the way the product does: an enclosure or frame wraps the stack, so no inner layer shows at the edges, and a front glass is dark and glossy with its printed border, not a light slab that shows the layers behind it.
- Parts render double-sided: back-face culling stays off and the shader turns every normal toward the camera, so one wrongly wound triangle can never open a see-through hole. This is the kit's job, not each example's.
- Every part is a closed solid. A tube has an outer wall, an inner wall and two end rings; a cone or reflector has a real wall thickness or end caps; nothing is a single open surface, so the crease lines and the SVG export stay clean. Check every part from front, back, both ends, above and below before shipping.
- The assembled state must look like the real object: parts nest where they really sit (cells inside the tube, lens inside the bezel, glass inside its frame), nothing pokes through a wall, and outer parts hide inner ones. Check the assembled state from the same six views.
- Materials are neutral lightness tiers, not colours: dark plastic, light plastic, metal, board. The open part takes the Ember gradient (amber at its top, coral at its base); every other part stays neutral. No text inside the 3D scene.
- The stage is the plain Workbench ground; the canvas draws only the plinth and the contact shadow over it.
- **Model in local frames.** A hinged or tilted sub-assembly (a laptop lid, a door on its hinge) is modelled upright in its own frame with the hinge at the origin, then placed with one helper that applies the same rotation to every primitive's position and orientation. Never rotate primitives one by one by hand. `GEOM.box(w, h, d)` is x × y × z; write the axis next to every size in the truth table so a 150 mm long speaker never becomes a 150 mm tall pillar.
- **Finish follows the product.** The surface finish says what the text says: a nano-texture or matte option renders matte (low spec), polished glass and logos glossy, anodised aluminium satin. If the popover mentions a finish, the material shows it.

## Interaction (the kit implements these; keep them)

- **The L-curve.** Scroll or pinch moves the camera along one L-shaped path: a vertical leg that descends from a bird's-eye view (elevation about 69°) to eye level (about 5°), a rounded corner, then a horizontal leg that moves in at eye level. Scrolling out runs the path back up. Each leg gets a fixed share of the scroll (descent 45 %, corner 15 %, approach 40 %) and is uniform by arc length inside it, so the close-up is not squeezed into the last few ticks.
- **Turn and tilt.** Left-drag sideways turns the object around its own vertical axis, with a little inertia; it never bobs up or down while turning, because the view is centred on a turn-invariant point. Dragging up and down tilts the view (elevation offset, clamped to 2°–85°) on top of the L-curve. Arrow keys left and right turn; up and down tilt while no part is open. Reset view clears the tilt.
- **Idle spin, always.** After 3 s without input the object turns slowly on its own axis (8°/s, eased in over a second), at whatever elevation, zoom, tilt and explode state the learner left it: from above, at eye level, close up, assembled or exploded. It continues from the current yaw, never snaps back to a default. Any input stops it; it resumes after the next 3 s of quiet. It pauses only while a part's popover is open, so the text and its leader line hold still, and reduced motion disables it.
- **Parts.** Hover lifts a part's lightness. Click opens its glass popover beside it: name, position (3 / 8), the job sentence, its numbers. The popover goes to the side of the open part that is free of the other parts: left or right of a vertical stack, above or below a horizontal row. It never covers another part of the object; check this with the projected bounds of all parts, not only the open one. Up and Down walk to the neighbouring part; Close, Esc or a click on empty space returns to the whole. The camera target eases to the open part, and it eases again when Up, Down or a click switches straight from one open part to another.
- **Assembled and exploded.** A segmented control in the docked toolbar (and the key E) moves the parts together or apart. Views open exploded.
- **Keyboard and screen readers.** Every action has a key and a button. The kit keeps a hidden ordered list of parts as buttons and announces the open part in a live region.

- **Export.** The toolbar's Export button saves the current view print-ready: always light colours on white, no plinth or shadow, cropped to the object, its labels and the title with a 48 px margin. In the exploded state every part gets its name in two label columns outside the object, with leader lines to the part. PNG at 4×. SVG is true vector: a GPU visibility pass (triangle ids) drops every fully hidden triangle, a BSP tree orders the rest back to front (plain depth sorting shreds layers 1 mm apart), crease lines are kept only where they are visible, and text stays text.

## Learning boundary

In a hint artifact, only parts the learner has already established carry their job and numbers. The others show as "Part 4" with no description in the HTML, the data or the accessible names. Opening a part does not unlock the next hint.

## Inspect

Look at the file in a browser: bird's-eye, the corner, eye level close up, one open popover, light and dark, reduced motion, a narrow viewport. Repeat the framing and popover checks at landscape (1280 × 720, 1920 × 1080), near-square (870 × 900) and portrait (390 × 844) windows, and walk every part with Up and Down down to the lowest one: each open part must sit fully inside the free area with its popover beside it. Check that the parts' order and numbers match the truth table. For a quick all-round check, capture contact sheets of four turns (0°, 90°, 180°, 270°) at bird's-eye and eye level, assembled and exploded, in both themes, and export one PNG and one SVG of the exploded view and compare them side by side.

## Record it

README previews and posts come from the real file: capture with the browser's screencast (every rendered frame with its timestamp), resample to a constant 30 fps (24 fps if the GIF would pass 8 MB), and encode at 960 px wide with a per-video palette. Start from a three-quarter view where the main face shows (a screen faces the camera, a door shows its outside), hold still long enough for the idle spin to start, open the part that carries the lesson for about three seconds, then show assembled and exploded once each.
